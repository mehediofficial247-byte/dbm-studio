Every client brief I get in 2026 asks for "minimal, but premium." Those two words get treated as synonyms, and they aren't. Minimal is a constraint on how much you show. Premium is a judgment on how well you show it. You can have a maximalist page that feels expensive, and a minimal one that feels cheap.

## What actually reads as luxury

The interfaces that read as luxury share a few traits:

- Generous, **intentional** whitespace instead of empty space left over by accident
- A type scale with real hierarchy, not just three sizes of the same weight
- Restraint in motion — one considered animation beats five scattered ones
- Consistent spacing, applied without exception

The fastest way to make a minimal design feel cheap is inconsistent spacing. An 8px grid, applied without exception, does more for perceived quality than any single visual flourish.

## A quick spacing scale

Here's the scale I default to on most projects — it maps cleanly onto Tailwind's spacing tokens:

| Token | Pixels | Use case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `space-2` | 8px | Icon-to-label gaps |
| `space-4` | 16px | Inline element spacing |
| `space-6` | 24px | Card padding |
| `space-12` | 48px | Section internal rhythm |
| `space-24` | 96px | Section-to-section |

## None of this is about having less content

It's about giving every remaining element more room to do its job. When a client asks for "more minimal," what they usually mean is "more considered" — and that's a hierarchy problem, not a content problem.
